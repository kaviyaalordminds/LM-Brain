Google Student Ambassador doesn't makes sense to me - Something important I want to bring to the attention of college students. Recently I’ve been seeing many students sharing posts about becoming a “Google Student Ambassador” and promoting programs where they are asked to scan QR codes, refer friends to try Gemini AI, and collect signups to earn certificates or swag. At first glance it sounds exciting. The name “Google Student Ambassador” itself carries a lot of weight. Naturally, many students assume this is an official Google campus program. But here is something most people don’t know. The original Google Student Ambassador Program actually existed years ago, but it was discontinued around 2016. Many of the programs circulating now under that same name, especially in India, appear to be third-party campaigns run by external partners to promote specific tools or trials. In many cases, students are asked to collect a large number of referrals or signups every month. That doesn’t automatically mean everything is a scam. But it does mean it’s not the same official Google-backed program students assume it is. And this is where I want to talk to you a little personally. If you are spending your time in college promoting something, chasing referral numbers, scanning QR codes in every classroom just to get a certificate or some swag… pause for a second and ask yourself something. What exactly am I learning from this? Because your college time is too valuable to spend it doing things that only look good on the surface but don’t really build you. If you genuinely want to be part of the Google ecosystem, there’s actually a much better path. Look into Google Developer Student Clubs (GDSC). This is something that’s officially connected with Google’s developer community. And more importantly, it focuses on building things, not just promoting things. You get to run workshops. You get to bring people together on campus. You get to explore AI, Cloud, Web, Android and actually build projects around them. And trust me, when a recruiter sees that you led a GDSC chapter or contributed actively, that speaks far louder than a referral-based ambassador certificate. So if you are someone who wants to grow, build, and really make use of your college years… choose things that help you create value, not just collect titles. Always verify first… and invest your time in things that actually help you grow. I am glad, in the last ten years we have built such a space where students can become part and build their life skills as a community of college students from different colleges of TN.If you'd like to become part of it, let me know!

**"Degrees are great, but skills are the real currency in the IT world."** Let’s face it: in the fast-paced realm of technology, a degree can feel like a ticket to a concert that’s already sold out. The truth is, while formal education lays the groundwork, it’s the hands-on skills that truly open doors to career growth and opportunities. Think of skill development like investing in the stock market. You wouldn’t put all your money into one stock and hope for the best, right? Instead, you diversify your portfolio, learning about different sectors and trends to maximize your returns. Similarly, in IT, the more skills you acquire, the more valuable you become in the job market. I remember mentoring a recent graduate who was struggling to land interviews despite having a solid GPA. After some discussions, we focused on enhancing his tech skills—specifically, coding and cloud computing. Within months, he transformed his profile, showcasing projects on GitHub and contributing to open-source initiatives. The result? He landed a job at a leading tech firm, proving that skill development can outshine traditional qualifications. So, why is skill development more crucial than degrees in IT? 1. **Real-World Application**: Employers are looking for candidates who can hit the ground running. Practical skills demonstrate your ability to solve real problems. 2. **Adaptability**: The tech landscape is ever-evolving. Continuous learning keeps you relevant and ready for the next big thing. 3. **Networking Opportunities**: Engaging in skill development often leads to collaborations, mentorships, and connections that can propel your career forward. **Actionable Takeaways:** - **Invest in Online Courses**: Platforms like Coursera, Udemy, or LinkedIn Learning offer courses tailored to the latest IT skills. Choose one and commit to completing it this month. - **Build a Portfolio**: Start a GitHub repository or a personal website to showcase your projects. This not only demonstrates your skills but also your passion for learning. - **Join Tech Communities**: Engage with fellow learners and professionals on platforms like GitHub, Stack Overflow, or local meetups. Networking can lead to job opportunities and collaborations. Remember, in the world of IT, it’s not just about what you know, but how you can apply it. The Washington Post ran a piece this morning on students speed-running online degrees. A North Carolina HR exec finished a bachelor's at Maine's YourPace program in three months, added a master's five weeks later, and now charges $99 to $199 to coach others through the same path. Educators are alarmed. Robin Hanson's reply cuts through it: either change how you measure learning, or let students show you they've already learned it. The alarm is the tell. A university degree was never really about the learning. I once asked Bryan Caplan which of my kids would have to deal with the university system. They're 0, 2, 7, and 13. He said all four. I'm not so sure. The question isn't whether a superior alternative appears. It's when the labor market stops treating the degree as the default signal. That transition is underway, and it's staggered. Universities have bundled two things: filtering who's capable, and issuing a credential everyone recognizes. AI breaks the filtering. Coursework that used to separate the top 20% of applicants from the rest is now an afternoon's work for a language model. Once the filter fails, the shared credential loses its grip. Employers notice first, institutions last. For the 13-year-old, enrolling around 2029, the fault line runs right through the decision. Degrees will still be priced like they matter while the signaling power is already hollowing out. Get the credential because institutional inertia is real, then pair it aggressively with visible work and output. Just doing school loses. For the 7-year-old, around 2036, the break is obvious. Online degrees are commoditized. Elite schools double down on scarcity and everyone else loses their moat. Hiring shifts toward direct capability assessment: live reasoning under observation, and whether someone can actually steer AI agents toward a useful result. Apprenticeships and portfolio-first hiring become first-class paths. For the 2-year-old, around 2041, the bundle is fully gone. Education and credentialing separate into different layers. AI tutors outperform classrooms at near-zero marginal cost. Reputation systems and verified assessments replace diplomas, and college becomes a niche signaling play or a social experience rather than an economic necessity. For my newborn, we're in post-scarcity education. The constraint isn't knowledge transfer anymore. It's attention and judgment, especially about which problems are worth solving. Environment design matters more than curriculum. The invariant across all four: use AI as leverage rather than avoiding it, ship visible work, perform under scrutiny you can't outsource, and develop taste about what's worth doing. Those hold up regardless of what replaces the university bundle. Caplan may still be right that all four end up with degrees. The degree just won't be doing what it used to.

Most study abroad platforms lose students at the form. Not at pricing. Not at program selection. At the form. A prospective student lands on the site ready to apply. Then they see it - one long scroll of fields. Personal info, academic history, preferences, budget, funding, career goals - all stacked on one page with no sense of where it ends. They scroll once. Close the tab. Never come back. That's not a traffic problem. That's a form problem. For Applyr, a Guided Application Engine for study abroad, we rebuilt onboarding around one idea: Only show what's needed, right when it's needed. We split the flow into 4 clear steps: → Personal Details → Academic Details → Study Preference → Budget Information. A stepper on the left shows the full path. A progress chip at the bottom moves from 17% → 32% → 60% → 98% as the student fills things in. Chips, dropdowns, and a budget slider replace typing wherever possible. Every screen asks for 4-6 fields max. Nothing more. The student always knows three things: Where they are. How much is left. What to do next. That's what turns a form into a flow and a drop-off into a submission. If you're building a SaaS where onboarding is the first real conversion moment, the form isn't a detail. It's the funnel. # Google Cloud for students

Advance your career by learning Google Cloud at your own pace, earning Cloud Certificates and skill badges, building projects, and connecting with student communities.

### Build your future with Google Cloud

### Learn at your own pace.

Complete the online program on your own terms. Learn in-demand skills on your schedule and advance your career.

### Prepare for in-demand roles.

In the U.S. alone, there are over 505,000 open entry-level roles 1 related to a Cloud Cybersecurity Analyst, with a median annual salary of $135,000.2 Make your resume competitive with credentials from Google.

### Join a supportive peer community.

Gain access to Developer Student Clubs or attend online events to get hands-on experience.

Your journey begins here

Google Cloud offers students access to a tailored learning program that lets them perform hands-on labs, earn badges, and demonstrate skills to employers in high-growth fields.

### Learn with Google Skills

Students can request access to free online labs, skill badges, and courses through Google Skills. Follow a defined plan or build and curate your own path to working and thriving in a cloud-first workplace.

*   200 free Google Skills credits
*   Credits expire one year after they are issued

[Request credits](https://services.google.com/fb/forms/googlecloudskillsbooststudenttrainingcreditsapplication/)

### Learn with Google Skills Arcade

Students can join the Google Skills Arcade by Google Cloud — a gamified space to learn and grow. Complete games and challenges, and earn badges that celebrate your progress!

*   No-cost access to hands-on labs
*   Real-world, hands-on Cloud skills with exclusive swag rewards

[Explore Google Skills Arcade](https://go.cloudskillsboost.google/arcade)

### Earn skill badges

A skill badge is an exclusive digital badge issued by Google Cloud in recognition of your proficiency with Google Cloud. Complete a series of hands-on labs and the challenge lab final assessment to receive a skill badge credential you can share with your network.

*   Demonstrate your technical skills
*   Post a skill badge to LinkedIn

[Learn about skill badges](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/training-certifications/new-google-cloud-skill-badges-learning-paths-and-remote-certification-opportunities)

### Earn a Google Cloud Certificate

Students who want to position themselves well to secure employment working with Google Cloud can go further by earning a shareable digital credential with Google Cloud Certificates.

*   Preview at no-cost on YouTube
*   No prerequisites required

[Explore certificates](https://cloud.google.com/learn/certificates)
